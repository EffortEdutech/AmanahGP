# Grant application intelligence for the Amanah Governance Platform

**The AGP has at least 12 viable grant pathways totalling over RM10 million in potential funding across Malaysian government agencies, Islamic institutions, and international bodies — and the timing could not be better.** The Malaysian government has explicitly identified waqf and zakat transparency as "industry pain points" awaiting technology solutions, PM Anwar Ibrahim personally championed blockchain-based waqf governance at the 2025 World Zakat and Waqf Forum, and MACC's escalating crackdown on NGO fraud (RM70 million in the Aman Palestin case alone) has made charity accountability a national priority. This report synthesises findings across all five research areas to provide AGP with a comprehensive evidence base for grant applications.

## 1. Grant landscape: 12 funding pathways ranked by fit

AGP's unique positioning at the intersection of Islamic social finance, govtech, and charity transparency makes it eligible for an unusually wide range of Malaysian funding programmes. The most promising pathways, ranked by alignment and grant size, are detailed below.

### Tier 1: Highest alignment (directly target Islamic fintech / social finance tech)

**Securities Commission FIKRA ACE Accelerator** is the single most aligned programme. This 6–8 week accelerator explicitly targets Islamic fintech solutions for "Social Finance Integration" — specifically waqf, zakat, and qard hassan. Partners include Bank Islam, Gobi Partners, Kenanga, and PNB. Benefits include structured mentorship, investor connections, POC/MVP testing with industry partners, and commercial arrangement opportunities. The new **FIKRALab** (launched March 2026) extends this into applied R&D anchored on Maqasid al-Shariah principles under the Capital Market Masterplan 2026–2030.

**MDEC Malaysia Digital Catalyst Grant (MDCG)** offers up to **RM1 million or 50% of project costs**. Critically, Islamic Digital Economy is one of nine promoted sectors, and Digital Finance is another — AGP sits squarely in both. The grant funds co-creation and commercialisation of innovative solutions with end-user partners. Duration is up to one year. **Prerequisite: Malaysia Digital (MD) Status**, which AGP should obtain immediately if not already held.

**IERIF (Islamic Economics Research and Innovation Fund)** at INCEIF University draws from a **RM20 million pool** (Budget 2024–2025 allocation), with over RM12 million already disbursed across 54 projects. Research themes explicitly include AI in Islamic finance, blockchain for waqf governance, and robo-advisory for zakat/waqf portfolios. Evaluation weights: research impact (35%), effectiveness and feasibility (25%), managerial capability (20%), budget and risk (20%).

### Tier 2: Strong fit (broader tech/social enterprise grants)

**Cradle Fund CIP Spark** provides up to **RM150,000** for pre-seed/MVP stage (18 months), while **CIP Sprint** offers up to **RM600,000** for seed-stage commercialisation (requires functional MVP). Applications are open year-round in batches; Batch 1 of 2026 has already completed pitching. Key eligibility for Sprint: Sdn Bhd ≤7 years old, accumulated revenue ≤RM5 million, 51% Malaysian-owned, minimum paid-up capital RM10,000. Cradle has funded over 1,100 startups contributing **RM1.83 billion** to national GDP since 2003.

**Yayasan Hasanah** offers three tiers. The **Hasanah Micro Grant 2026** (up to **RM50,000**, currently open with rolling quarterly reviews) suits pilot-stage work. The **Hasanah Special Grant** scales up to **RM2.5 million** for projects spanning 3+ years — this is the largest single-source grant available to AGP. The **HSEF 2025** (up to RM100,000) targets social enterprises. Eligibility requires minimum 2 years operational history and audited financials. AGP aligns with Hasanah's Knowledge and Community Development impact areas and its core values of Accountability, Trust, and Innovation.

**MDEC Malaysia Digital Acceleration Grant (MDAG)** reaches up to **RM5 million or 70% of costs** for scaling MD-status companies into regional champions. The newer **MDAG-AI** variant caps at **RM2 million** for AI solution commercialisation aligned with the National AI Roadmap.

### Tier 3: International Islamic institution funding

The **IsDB Transform Fund** (US$500 million) provides **US$50,000 to US$1 million** per project through periodic Calls for Innovation via the IsDB Engage platform. Malaysia qualifies as a founding member country. The **IsDB Tadamon** platform offers grants up to **US$40,000** per CSO through its Accelerator programme — 50 CSOs selected from 350 applications across 28 OIC countries in recent rounds. The **IsDB Institute Islamic Finance Grants Program** approved 14 projects worth US$2.3 million in 2023 alone, covering topics from Awqaf Free Zones to Islamic Finance AI Assistants.

### Tier 4: Supplementary and conditional pathways

**TERAJU SUPERB** (up to **RM500,000**) requires 60% Bumiputera ownership. **MRANTI MICA** provides RM150,000 in support for social impact startups. The **MOSTI National Technology & Innovation Sandbox (NTIS)** has a **RM100 million** allocation for testing products in live environments. **BNM's Fintech Regulatory Sandbox** offers a 12-month testing period if AGP incorporates regulated financial services. **SME Corp BEEP** provides 50% matching grants up to RM500,000 for Bumiputera SMEs.

## 2. The statistics that make AGP's case undeniable

### Zakat: a RM4+ billion system demanding transparency

Malaysia's zakat ecosystem has grown to staggering proportions. National collection reached approximately **RM3.65 billion in 2022** and has been growing at 10–12% annually. By state, Selangor's Lembaga Zakat Selangor (LZS) collected a record **RM1.22 billion in 2024**, distributing RM1.19 billion to over 370,000 asnaf — a 97.5% distribution rate. Federal Territories' PPZ-MAIWP collected **RM1.133 billion in 2024** (10% year-on-year growth), with 1.84 million zakat payers. Perak hit a record RM253 million. Digital channels are surging: LZS saw direct debit payments grow 60% in 2024, while PPZ-MAIWP reported **73% growth in cryptocurrency zakat payments**.

Distribution efficiency varies dramatically between states — Selangor distributes 98%, Federal Territories approximately 84%, and Kedah only about 70% — highlighting precisely the transparency gap AGP addresses.

### NGOs: nearly 100,000 organisations with no centralised oversight

As of August 2024, Malaysia had **96,722 registered NGOs** (85,984 main organisations plus 10,738 branches) under the Registrar of Societies (ROS). Annual registration has been running at 8,000–12,000 new applications per year. There is no centralised Charities Commission (unlike the UK), no mandatory public portal for audited financials, and the governing legislation — the Societies Act 1966 and House-to-House and Street Collections Act 1947 — is widely considered obsolete for modern online fundraising.

### Mosques and surau: 25,000+ venues ripe for digitalisation

JAKIM data records **6,836 mosques and 18,360 surau** as of end 2022, totalling approximately 25,196 Islamic institutions. Sabah has the most mosques (1,141), while Selangor leads in surau (2,170). The UsimMasjidPro digital mosque platform has reached only 500 of these — less than 2% penetration — indicating massive room for digital governance adoption.

### Waqf: trillions in potential, held back by governance gaps

JAWHAR's most current data (July 2024) records **18,402 lots covering approximately 20,993 hectares** of waqf land, of which 84% is utilised by lot count. However, this figure conflicts with widely cited earlier data suggesting only 12–13% of waqf land is developed, and the government's own Digital Investment Office acknowledges that waqf assets estimated at over **RM1.3 trillion** remain largely underleveraged. The disconnect between these figures itself underscores the governance and data transparency problem AGP targets.

### The fraud crisis: MACC enforcement validates AGP's mission

MACC's crackdown on NGO fraud has intensified dramatically. The **Aman Palestin case** (February 2024) saw its chairman and CEO charged with **164 counts of CBT, cheating, and money laundering totalling RM39.53 million** — with alleged total misappropriation reaching **RM70 million**. The **Pertubuhan Kebajikan Serantau Muslim case** (Op Serantau, June 2025) involved alleged misappropriation of **RM26 million** in public donations. In March 2026, MACC confirmed investigations into more than 5 NGOs raising funds from the public, including groups collecting donations in the name of Palestine. MACC made **1,334 arrests** in 2024 overall, and the Multi-Agency Task Force confiscated **RM1.6 billion** in funds and assets. These cases provide AGP with powerful, specific evidence that the problem it solves is real, urgent, and recognised by enforcement authorities.

### Digital giving: explosive growth creates the infrastructure

Malaysia's digital payment ecosystem provides the infrastructure for AGP's adoption. E-payment transactions hit **18.4 billion in 2025** (25% growth), reaching 538 transactions per capita. DuitNow QR transactions doubled to **3 billion in 2025** across 3 million merchant touchpoints. Malaysia ranks **second globally in QR code payment adoption at 61.5%**, behind only China. E-wallet adoption stands at 64% of the population. This digital readiness means Malaysians are already accustomed to digital financial transactions — the barrier to adopting digital charity governance is cultural and institutional, not technological.

## 3. Comparable funded projects that validate AGP's model

Several comparable platforms have successfully secured funding, providing AGP with both benchmarks and differentiation opportunities.

**Masverse's e-Wakalah blockchain zakat platform** is the closest Malaysian comparable. Backed by MDEC's Blockchain Pilot Programme and partnered with Lembaga Zakat Selangor, this proof-of-concept used purpose-bound money (PBM) to ensure tokenised zakat was digitally programmed for designated recipients, with every transaction immutable and auditable on MasChain. The POC was completed May–December 2024 with successful verification. This validates both the technology approach and MDEC's willingness to fund Islamic social finance blockchain projects.

**Ethis Group / GlobalSadaqah** raised RM6.8 million in a pre-Series A round and was awarded the first Shariah-compliant Equity Crowdfunding licence by the Securities Commission. Its subsidiary GlobalSadaqah — an award-winning CSR, zakat, and waqf management platform — demonstrates market appetite for Islamic charity transparency tech. Ethis is now collaborating with Gobi Partners on a US$20 million seed fund for Shariah-compliant startups globally.

**Indonesia's BAZNAS digital transformation** provides regional proof at scale. Digital zakat channels grew from 1% to 15–20% of collections, with big data-based mustahik mapping achieving 70% accuracy improvement and operational cost reductions of 40%. Indonesia's **Kitabisa.com** secured US$5 million from the International Finance Corporation (IFC/World Bank Group), proving that international development finance institutions will invest in charity transparency platforms — Kitabisa collected IDR 871.9 billion (~US$60M) in 2020, surpassing all traditional zakat institutions.

**UsimMasjidPro** has registered 500 mosques since launch, with government endorsement from the Minister in PM's Department (Religious Affairs). Its digital infaq module with QRPay and financial transparency features represents the mosque digitalisation segment AGP could complement or integrate with.

The **IsDB Tadamon platform** itself demonstrates institutional appetite: a US$21 million programme empowering CSOs across 57 OIC countries through crowdfunding, capacity building, and technology. The platform has registered over 1,000 CSOs and trained 658+ participants through its Crowdfunding Academy.

**Key success factors** across all funded projects: regulatory alignment with SC/BNM/MDEC frameworks, government institutional partnerships (especially with state zakat boards), transparency as a core value proposition, blockchain/AI technology innovation, SDG alignment (especially SDG 16), Shariah compliance certification, demonstrated scalability across OIC countries, and quantified social impact metrics.

## 4. Policy alignment: AGP as a direct instrument of national strategy

AGP can position itself as a direct implementation tool for at least six major government policy frameworks, with specific citations for each.

### Malaysia Madani and Ekonomi MADANI

The MADANI framework's core pillars map directly to AGP's mission. **Amanah (Trust)** is literally a MADANI pillar — and AGP's name. **Daya Cipta (Innovation)** supports digital transformation for social impact. The framework is explicitly rooted in maqasid al-shariah principles. The **Wakaf MADANI initiative** targets 20% growth of the Islamic social finance sector by 2030, and the government has established Permodalan Wakaf Nasional Bhd (PWNB) with RM5 million initial capital. Budget 2026 introduced **tokenised cash waqf sukuk** — a new digital instrument channelling public contributions into social infrastructure — demonstrating direct government appetite for digital Islamic social finance innovation.

### 12th and 13th Malaysia Plans

The 12MP Mid-Term Review stated: "Steps to reform and expand Islamic finance will be accelerated for Malaysia to be a leader in the global Islamic economy" and identified waqf funds as "a new approach to developing the Islamic economy." The **13th Malaysia Plan (2026–2030)** allocates **RM611 billion** across 27 strategic priorities, with explicit emphasis on leveraging Malaysia's Islamic finance hub status, advancing the AI Nation Framework, and implementing Government-as-a-Platform approaches. GNI per capita target is RM77,200 by 2030.

### MyDIGITAL Blueprint

Strategic Thrust 5 (Inclusive Digital Society) and Thrust 6 (Trusted, Secure, and Ethical Digital Environment) directly support AGP's mission. The target of digital economy contributing over 25% of GDP by 2030, with 5,000 startups and digital banking licences (including 2 Islamic digital banks) already issued, creates the ecosystem AGP operates within.

### BNM Value-Based Intermediation and Financial Sector Blueprint

BNM's Financial Sector Blueprint 2022–2026 Strategic Thrust 5 is explicitly to "advance value-based finance through Islamic finance leadership." In 2024, Islamic Financial Institutions intermediated **RM148.6 billion** into VBI-aligned activities, with 79% in social finance. Islamic financing now represents **48% of total financing** in Malaysia. AGP enables Islamic institutions to demonstrate VBI compliance by providing transparent tracking of social finance outcomes.

### The government's own stated need

Perhaps most powerfully, the Malaysian Digital Investment Office website explicitly states: **"Transparency and accountability of waqf and zakat are industry pain points. Technology solutions providers have opportunities to unlock the economic potential of waqf and zakat through digital adoption initiatives."** This is the government inviting solutions like AGP.

### Budget allocations to cite

Budget 2026 allocated **RM2.6 billion** for Islamic propagation and development, **RM53 million** for the Malaysia Digital Acceleration Grant (blockchain, AI, quantum computing), and RM150 million for pondok institution infrastructure. Budget 2025 allocated RM2 billion for Islamic affairs and RM50 million for the MSME Digital Grant MADANI. The IERIF received RM20 million specifically for Islamic economics research and innovation.

## 5. Grant writing strategy: what evaluators actually want

### The Maqasid al-Shariah framework is non-negotiable

For any Islamic institution grant, AGP must map its features to the five objectives of Shariah. **Hifz al-Deen** (preservation of religion): AGP strengthens trust in Islamic charitable institutions, protecting the integrity of religious giving. **Hifz al-Maal** (preservation of wealth): transparent management of waqf and zakat assets prevents leakage. **Hifz al-Nafs** (preservation of life): better aid distribution reaches those in genuine need. **Hifz al-Aql** (preservation of intellect): data-driven insights enable evidence-based governance decisions. **Hifz al-Nasl** (preservation of progeny): sustainable charitable infrastructure secures giving for future generations. The Securities Commission issued formal Maqasid Al-Shariah Guidance for the Islamic Capital Market in October 2023, giving this framework institutional weight.

### Evidence hierarchy for Malaysian evaluators

The strongest evidence types, in order: pilot data showing actual user adoption and transaction volumes; **letters of support from State Islamic Religious Councils (SIRCs)** — these are critical because SIRCs are the sole trustees of waqf under state jurisdiction; MOUs with JAWHAR, JAKIM, or Yayasan Wakaf Malaysia; endorsement from INCEIF or ISRA for academic credibility; partnership commitments from Islamic banks (Bank Islam, Maybank Islamic, Bank Rakyat); user testimonials from asnaf and B40 beneficiaries; and quantified social impact metrics (funds tracked, beneficiaries served, governance improvements measured).

### Language and framing that resonates

A bilingual approach works best: primary documents in English for broader reach, with executive summary and key sections in Bahasa Malaysia for government evaluators. Use MADANI vocabulary — frame everything using the pillars of Sustainability, Amanah, Innovation, and Ihsan. Deploy "rakyat-centric" language (empowering the people). Integrate Islamic terminology naturally — maqasid al-shariah, maslahah (public interest), VBI, amanah — with clear English explanations. Reference specific policy documents by name and section. Avoid purely Western social enterprise framing; instead blend Islamic principles with conventional frameworks like Theory of Change, SROI, and SDG mapping.

### Common rejection reasons to avoid

Applications fail when they miss minimum eligibility criteria (SSM registration, equity requirements, minimum paid-up capital); submit generic proposals not tailored to Malaysian policy context; lack financial sustainability plans beyond the grant period; present no prototype or user testing data; ignore Shariah governance requirements (no Shariah advisory board); fail to address the state-federal waqf jurisdiction complexity (waqf is state-level under SIRCs, not federal); field teams without Islamic finance or sufficient technical expertise; or set unrealistic KPIs without supporting pilot evidence.

## Recommended application sequence and immediate actions

AGP should pursue grants in a deliberate sequence that builds credibility at each stage.

**Immediate actions (Month 1–2):** Apply for Malaysia Digital (MD) Status from MDEC — this unlocks most MDEC grants and tax incentives. Submit to the **Yayasan Hasanah Micro Grant 2026** (RM50,000, currently open, rolling reviews) for pilot funding. Register on the **IsDB Engage platform** and monitor for the next Transform Fund Call for Innovation.

**Short-term (Month 2–4):** Apply to **Cradle Fund CIP Spark** (RM150,000) if at MVP stage, or **CIP Sprint** (RM600,000) if the MVP is ready for commercial validation. Apply to the **SC FIKRA ACE Accelerator** for the next cohort — this provides mentorship, industry connections, and credibility that strengthens all subsequent applications. Approach **JAWHAR** directly as a technology partnership proposal for standardising ICT across State Islamic Religious Councils.

**Medium-term (Month 4–8):** With MD Status secured and pilot data from the Hasanah grant, apply for **MDEC MDCG** (up to RM1 million). Submit a research collaboration proposal to **IERIF/INCEIF** (RM20 million pool). Apply to the **Yayasan Hasanah Special Grant** (up to RM2.5 million) with the pilot results, SIRC endorsement letters, and a comprehensive Theory of Change.

**Longer-term (Month 8–12):** Target **MDAG** (up to RM5 million) for regional scaling. Submit to **IsDB Transform Fund** (up to US$1 million) positioning AGP as replicable across 57 OIC member countries. Each successful grant builds the evidence base and credibility for the next, creating a compounding cycle of funding and impact.

The window is open. The government has named the problem, enforcement actions have created urgency, digital infrastructure is mature, and multiple funding programmes are actively seeking exactly what AGP offers. The question is not whether AGP can find funding — it is how quickly AGP can position itself to capture it.
